Condorcet wrote the founding document of progress while hiding from the Terror, and died in custody before it was published. Plus what is still unknown about how Condorcet died.

Key ideas
  • Condorcet’s claim: human perfectibility is indefinite, argued across nine past epochs and one prophesied tenth. In his words, “nature has fixed no limits to our hopes.”
  • The tenth epoch’s three hopes: the end of inequality between nations, the progress of equality within them, and the real improvement of man, including pushing death back. He says man will not become immortal, only that death may come from “extraordinary accidents” or “the slow and gradual decay of the vital powers.”
  • The text is stranger than its reputation. It condemns “our sanguinary contempt for men of a different complexion or a different creed” and calls for equal rights between the sexes, while also sketching a European-led “civilizing” mission.
  • Malthus’s objection: population grows geometrically and food arithmetically, and naming no limit is not the same as proving there isn’t one.
  • Berlin’s objection: values don’t harmonize under reason. Liberty and equality collide, and a system that assumes otherwise keeps rediscovering the conflict.
  • Where the episode lands: Malthus found the real weakness in the tenth epoch, but both of his curves bent. Birth rates fell as societies got richer and more educated, and food production outran his arithmetic.

0:00In the summer of seventeen ninety-three, a wanted man sat in a back room on the rue Servandoni in Paris, writing a history of the future. Outside, the Revolution was arresting his friends. He had no books to check and no reason to think he’d live to see the year out. What he wrote, in a hurry, was the most confident prediction anyone has ever made about where humanity is going.

0:25This is A Genealogy of Technological Optimism, episode four of one hundred. We’re in Act One: Why should history go anywhere?

0:36The man in the back room was Nicolas de Condorcet, pronounced kon-dor-SAY. He was fifty years old, a mathematician famous for a theorem about juries, a marquis who’d thrown in with the Revolution, and now a Girondin with a warrant on his head, hiding in the house of a Madame Vernet. The manuscript would be published after his death as the Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind. It’s the founding document of the idea that history has a direction, and that the direction is up.

1:11The Sketch divides the past into nine epochs. Tribal society, then herding, then farming, then alphabetic writing. Greece to Alexander. The division of the sciences, their long decline, the Crusades, the revival of learning, printing, and finally the age from Descartes to the French Republic, when science threw off authority. Nine epochs of the past, each one generating the prejudices the next one had to clear away. And then the tenth epoch, which hasn’t happened yet. Condorcet writes it as prophecy, and he reduces the whole future to three hopes. The end of inequality between nations. The progress of equality within them. And what he calls the real improvement of man, by which he means, among other things, that we might push death itself back. Not abolish it. He says man will not become immortal. But death, he writes, will become nothing more than the effect of extraordinary accidents, or of the slow and gradual decay of the vital powers.

2:16That’s the argument, and the word he uses for it is perfectibility. Improvement without a fixed limit. Nature, he writes, has fixed no limits to our hopes. He’d spent his career building the mathematics for it: life tables, voting systems, probability applied to human affairs. He believed uncertainty itself could be calculated. Bacon again, a hundred and sixty-six years on. Salomon’s House promised the effecting of all things possible. Condorcet turns the promise into a timetable.

2:49Now what’s actually in the book, because the reputation says naive and the text says otherwise. The middle epochs are a millennium-long indictment of the Christian church for strangling inquiry. And the tenth epoch contains passages that don’t sound like a complacent European at all. He denounces, in his words, our monopolies, our treachery, our sanguinary contempt for men of a different complexion or a different creed. He calls for the total annihilation of the prejudices that established between the sexes an inequality of rights. He’d argued for women’s citizenship three years earlier, in seventeen ninety, and Mary Wollstonecraft had published her Vindication in seventeen ninety-two. But almost no one building a grand theory of progress put either cause at the center of the future. The tenth epoch also proposes free public education, a universal language, and insurance against old age.

3:47The tension sits inside the same pages. The man who condemns conquest also writes that Europeans will carry liberty to Africa and Asia, and he describes the barbarity of African tribes in terms no anti-colonialist would use. He wants the empire of reason to replace the empires of kings. It’s still an empire, and it’s still administered from Paris. His vehicle was commerce and instruction rather than conquest, which softens the method without changing the direction. The Sketch contains both the condemnation of colonialism and the blueprint for a subtler kind of it.

4:24Condorcet never saw the book in print. In March of seventeen ninety-four he left his hiding place, was arrested at Clamart, a suburb southwest of Paris, and was found dead in his cell at Bourg-la-Reine two days later. Some accounts say he was guillotined. He wasn’t. The usual account says poison, possibly supplied by a friend. One recent historian argues murder. Natural causes haven’t been ruled out either. We still don’t know how he died. His widow, Sophie de Grouchy, got the manuscript into print in seventeen ninety-five.

5:01The first readers split the way you’d expect. The royalist Joseph de Maistre attacked it within two years as the work of an enemy sect. But the attack that mattered came from an English clergyman who read the tenth epoch and saw a hole in it. Thomas Malthus published his Essay on the Principle of Population in seventeen ninety-eight, and the first chapter names Condorcet directly. His objection is simple and it hasn’t gone away. Population, left alone, grows geometrically. Food grows arithmetically. So even in Condorcet’s perfected future, the numbers catch up within a generation or two, and misery returns through what Malthus calls the inevitable laws of nature. He called the perfectibility argument, in his phrase, in the highest degree unphilosophical. Naming no limit isn’t proving there isn’t one. Forty years later a young Darwin read the Essay and took the struggle for existence from its pages.

6:02The economics aren’t the only problem. Condorcet’s tenth epoch assumes that all good things converge, that liberty, equality, and knowledge pull in the same direction under reason’s guidance. Isaiah Berlin, two centuries later, called this the monist faith, and his objection is that values don’t harmonize. They collide. The freedom to differ and the equality of outcome are both good, and you can’t maximize both. A system built on the assumption that reason dissolves the conflict will keep rediscovering the conflict, and each time it will look like a failure of reason rather than a fact about values. His lecture Two Concepts of Liberty came in nineteen fifty-eight, after the century’s experiments.

6:49I think Malthus found the real weakness in the tenth epoch. Finite land can’t feed exponential growth forever. But his two curves both bent. Birth rates collapsed as societies got richer and more educated, the demographic transition neither man foresaw. France’s own birth rate started falling within decades of his death. And food production broke clean through the arithmetic assumption.

7:14The afterlife is where Condorcet’s tenth epoch quietly won. Auguste Comte built positivism on it, the doctrine that societies pass through stages toward the scientific one. The nineteenth century’s whole idea of progress, the twentieth century’s modernization theory, the stages-of-growth charts. All of it descends from those three hopes, and those charts still show up in emerging-markets pitch decks. Comte’s followers even built a church on it, with a calendar of secular saints. It didn’t last. Bacon’s men in secret deciding what mankind may know became Condorcet’s open future that mankind may choose. Fedorov again: he wanted to reassemble the dead from scattered particles. Condorcet asked for less. He wanted us to stop dying so stupidly early.

8:12It was written in a hurry, in a back room, without books to check.
