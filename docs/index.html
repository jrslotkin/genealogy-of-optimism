The series

An intellectual genealogy of technological optimism in 100 books, ordered as an argument in ten acts. It starts with the theology the idea of progress grew out of and the psychology of the people who act on it. It then moves through how knowledge grows, how markets and institutions turn knowledge into growth, how innovation is built and financed, and the attempt to engineer away every remaining constraint.

About three quarters of the books make the case. The other quarter are the strongest arguments against it. Each critic sits next to the claims it attacks, and critiques of technology as such are held for the end.

Key ideas
  • Fedorov’s claim: death is the single root of every human problem, and humanity’s common task is the physical resurrection of all the dead, achieved by human scientific action, not divine miracle.
  • The machinery of the argument: non-kindred relations, sonship as well as brotherhood, supramoralism, the museum as the institution of memory, and the regulation of nature, including space travel to recover the ancestors’ particles and house the resurrected.
  • The book was never published in Fedorov’s lifetime; two disciples assembled it from notes, drafts, and essays into posthumous volumes in 1906 and 1913. Its first readers included Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, and Solovyov.
  • The strongest objection is Bulgakov’s: the project never answers what life and death are, so reassembled matter cannot restore the person, and no laboratory can reach what is more than matter.
  • Where it lands: the engineering failed, but the provocation stands. Tsiolkovsky carried the space-travel logic into rocketry, transhumanism and cryonics took up the lineage, and every theory of progress still has to answer for the dead it writes off.
Read the episode · 7 min

0:00In the reading room of Moscow’s Rumyantsev Museum, late in the nineteenth century, there was a librarian whom strangers mistook for a beggar. His coat was a rag. He ate hard rolls and drank tea, gave away his salary to students, and walked everywhere, even in winter. He had read everything, and he believed every book in the building was evidence for a single charge against humanity: that we have accepted death.

0:29This is A Genealogy of Technological Optimism, episode two of one hundred. We’re in Act One: Why should history go anywhere?

0:37His name was Nikolai Fedorov, born in eighteen twenty-nine, the illegitimate son of a Russian prince and a peasant woman. He taught in provincial schools and became an assistant librarian, where he stayed for twenty-five years. Readers received not only the books they requested but everything else he thought necessary. The young Konstantin Tsiolkovsky studied under his direction for three years. Tolstoy admired his asceticism. Dostoevsky praised his logic. He was known, to those who knew him, as the Socrates of Moscow. He published almost nothing in his lifetime, partly from opposition to copyright, and partly because he saw writing as a prelude to action. When he died in nineteen oh three, two disciples gathered his notes, drafts, and essays into two volumes. The first appeared in nineteen oh six under the title The Philosophy of the Common Task.

1:33The claim is as stark as any in philosophy. Death, Fedorov argues, is not a fact to be accepted or a mystery to be contemplated. It is the single root of every human problem, and every solution that leaves it untouched is a fraud. War, poverty, disease, the whole catalogue of suffering: all of it flows from mortality, and all of it ends when mortality ends. The task he sets is the physical resurrection of everyone who has ever lived, achieved not by God at the end of time but by human beings, through science. This is what he means by the common task, and it is not a metaphor. In a letter to one of Fedorov’s followers in eighteen seventy-eight, Dostoevsky asked the question: is this meant literally, will the dead be raised, quote, not only in our minds, not allegorically, but in fact, in person, actually, in bodies. The answer, from Fedorov’s writings, is an emphatic yes.

2:36To understand what he is proposing, you need his moral psychology. We receive life from our fathers, Fedorov says, and we repay the debt by forgetting them. Modern life is built on what he calls non-kindred relations: the living alienated from each other, and all of the living alienated from the dead. Progress itself, in his account, is an accusation. It makes ancestors into the accused and descendants into judges, sacrificing the fathers for the comfort of the sons. Against this he sets sonship as well as brotherhood: we owe our existence to those who came before, and the debt can only be repaid by giving life back. Christianity, in his reading, is not a set of beliefs about the afterlife. It is a project. Christ showed what was to be done, in the raising of Lazarus, but not how. The how is ours to discover. This is why he called his philosophy supramoralism: morality raised to the level of a cosmic engineering project. The museum becomes in his writing the central institution of the future, because the museum keeps humanity’s memory of the dead, and memory is the first step toward restoration.

3:54The mechanics are where the philosophy turns strange, and prophetic. Death is disintegration, the scattering of the particles that made up a person. But those particles cannot leave finite space, so they can in principle be found, gathered, and reassembled. The catch is that the Earth is too small a workshop. To recover the dust of the ancestors, Fedorov writes, research teams will have to travel to the moon, the planets, and the distant points of the universe, and the resurrected will need those worlds to live on, their bodies perhaps rebuilt to survive conditions no human body could now endure. Space travel is therefore not an adventure but a moral necessity, built into the logic of the project. Along the way he sketches a program that reads like a checklist from a later century: regulation of the weather, solar energy in place of fossil fuels, the Earth managed as an ecosystem rather than exploited as a mine, and failing human organs replaced with artificial ones.

5:00In his own lifetime almost no one read him, because there was almost nothing to read. His influence traveled by conversation, in that museum reading room. Dostoevsky wrote in his Writer’s Diary that he had rarely read anything more logical. The philosopher Vladimir Solovyov wrote to Fedorov in eighteen eighty-one that he accepted the project unconditionally, calling him teacher and spiritual father. Tolstoy, who could never live up to his own ascetic ideals, revered a man who did. After Fedorov’s death the disciples’ volumes found a real audience. Fedorovism became a force in the intellectual ferment of pre-revolutionary Russia and the early Soviet years. Stalin’s purges of the nineteen thirties suppressed it, and in the West it barely registered. Frederick Copleston’s four-hundred-and-forty-five-page history of Russian philosophy mentions Fedorov twice, both times in footnotes.

5:53Now the turn, judged first by what Fedorov and his first readers could know. The strongest objection available in his own century was theological, put most sharply by the Orthodox theologian Sergei Bulgakov. In Christian doctrine, resurrection is God’s work, promised at the end of time. Fedorov makes it man’s work, scheduled for the foreseeable future. Bulgakov’s charge is that the project never answers its own first question: what is life, and what is death. If a person is only matter, then reassembling the particles cannot restore the person, because the continuity that made them who they were is gone. If a person is more than matter, then no laboratory can reach what needs to be raised. Either way, the method cannot do what the promise requires. A second objection needs no theology. Fedorov never asks whether the dead want to come back. He assumes their consent, but the project is a rescue operation mounted on behalf of people who never asked to be rescued. And a third: the common task demands the whole of humanity united in a single project, directed, in Fedorov’s own writings, by the Russian Czar. A plan that requires universal agreement and a single command has a failure mode, and the twentieth century supplied it more than once.

7:18With a century of hindsight, the picture changes, and not only against him. Start with what landed. The teenager who studied in his reading room, Tsiolkovsky, became the theorist of rocket flight, and the Soviet space program that put Sputnik overhead in nineteen fifty-seven grew in soil Fedorov had tilled. The historian Brian Harvey has shown how deep that influence ran: for Soviet citizens, Sputnik was less a shock than the realization of an old idea. The artificial organs, the solar energy, the managed Earth: all mainstream now, all proposed by a librarian in the eighteen eighties. The stranger afterlife is in the life-extension movement. Russian transhumanists founded their movement in two thousand three in his lineage, and the country’s first cryonics company, KrioRus, began freezing bodies in two thousand five on the wager that future science will do what Fedorov said it must. The physicist Frank Tipler, working independently, arrived at a cosmology ending with the resurrection of everyone who ever lived, simulated rather than reassembled. Fedorov would have recognized the ambition and quarreled with the method, which is to say he would have recognized it.

8:33But hindsight also sharpens the objections. The historian Michael Hagemeister has argued that Russian cosmism, the movement Fedorov supposedly founded, is partly a retrospective construction, assembled in the late Soviet period to serve a nationalist story about Russian identity. If he is right, then Fedorov’s influence is real but messier than the legend: less a school than a rumor that kept finding new carriers. And the deepest problem remains the one Bulgakov named. A century of biology has not made the reassembly of persons from scattered particles more plausible. What survives, then, is not the engineering. It is the moral provocation at the center: that every theory of progress writes off the dead, and that a progress worth the name would have to answer to them.

9:23The only portrait of Fedorov was drawn in secret, by Leonid Pasternak, in the library, because the sitter would never have agreed to pose. A thin man in a ragged coat, surrounded by books, looking up. The scholar George Young, who spent years studying him, wrote that what we should do with our Godlike power is a question that someone is going to have to answer, and that Fedorov’s answer may not be the best proposed, but it is the most thorough and the deepest attempt at one.

The ten acts

Syllabus (PDF)
IWhy should history go anywhere? 1–10
  1. 1The Phenomenon of ManPierre Teilhard de Chardin1955ListenA Jesuit paleontologist reads evolution as one movement toward greater complexity and consciousness, ending in a planetary mind. The list opens with progress at its most cosmic, stated as destiny.
  2. 2The Philosophy of the Common TaskNikolai Fedorov1906ListenA Moscow librarian argues that humanity’s shared moral duty is to defeat death and resurrect every ancestor through science. Tsiolkovsky studied in his reading room, and Russian cosmism starts here.
  3. 3New AtlantisFrancis Bacon1626Salomon’s House is a detailed blueprint for a state-funded research institution, written before one existed. A template for the Royal Society and, at some distance, for every lab since.
  4. 4Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human MindNicolas de Condorcet1795Written in hiding during the Terror, shortly before he died in custody. Argues that improvement in knowledge, equality, and even lifespan has no fixed limit.
  5. 5History of the Idea of ProgressRobert Nisbet1980Traces progress from the Greeks through Augustine to the moderns and argues it has always rested on something close to faith. Sets up the dispute in the next two books.
  6. 6Meaning in HistoryKarl Löwith1949The charge: modern faith in progress is Christian eschatology with God removed. Worth holding against the way AGI is now described.
  7. 7The Legitimacy of the Modern AgeHans Blumenberg1966The reply to Löwith. Curiosity and human self-assertion are modernity’s own achievements and owe nothing to the theology they replaced; the most rigorous defense of progress on its own terms.
  8. 8The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of CapitalismMax Weber1905Capitalist discipline grew out of religious anxiety about salvation. It ends with the iron cage, a system that keeps running after the belief that built it has gone.
  9. 9The Spirit of Democratic CapitalismMichael Novak1982A Catholic theologian’s case that democratic capitalism is a moral and spiritual achievement. The theological defense its critics seldom engage.
  10. 10Frankenstein; or, The Modern PrometheusMary Shelley1818A maker abandons what he made, and the creation asks what it is owed. The question the rest of the list keeps returning to.
IIWhat kind of creature is building the future? 11–20
  1. 11Thus Spoke ZarathustraFriedrich Nietzsche1883Man as something to be overcome. Transhumanism and accelerationism both draw on it, usually without saying so.
  2. 12The Denial of DeathErnest Becker1973Civilization as an immortality project built to manage the fear of death. Explains a great deal about why people build, and raises a hard question about longevity research.
  3. 13Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the WorldRené Girard, with Jean-Michel Oughourlian and Guy Lefort1978Mimetic desire and the scapegoat mechanism, set out as dialogues. Thiel encountered Girard at Stanford, and Zero to One’s view of competition comes from here.
  4. 14Notes from UndergroundFyodor Dostoevsky1864A narrator who would rather act against his own interest than live as a piano key in a rationally planned world. The first serious argument that people will reject a utopia designed for their benefit.
  5. 15Thinking, Fast and SlowDaniel Kahneman2011The catalog of systematic error in human judgment. Read the priming chapters with the replication record in mind; the core heuristics-and-biases findings have held up better.
  6. 16Rationality for MortalsGerd Gigerenzer2008Under real uncertainty, simple heuristics often outperform optimization. Closer to how founders and investors actually decide, and a direct challenge to Kahneman’s framing.
  7. 17The Secret of Our SuccessJoseph Henrich2015Humans succeed through accumulated culture, and innovation scales with the size and connectedness of the population sharing it. The anthropological base for why networks and cities matter later.
  8. 18Origins of GeniusDean Keith Simonton1999Creativity treated as Darwinian variation and selection. The best predictor of a creator’s hits is the number of attempts, the same distribution venture portfolios live in.
  9. 19Expert Political JudgmentPhilip E. Tetlock2005Two decades of expert forecasts scored against outcomes. Foxes beat hedgehogs, and simple statistical extrapolation beat most of the experts.
  10. 20FoundationIsaac Asimov1951Placed right after Tetlock: a mathematician who can forecast the fall of a galactic civilization. Psychohistory has had a long afterlife among people who build companies.
IIIHow can a creature like that know anything? 21–28
  1. 21Science and the Modern WorldAlfred North Whitehead1925Names the method of invention as the nineteenth century’s greatest invention. Also introduces the fallacy of misplaced concreteness, a warning against mistaking models for the world.
  2. 22Conjectures and RefutationsKarl Popper1963Knowledge grows through bold guesses and criticism, never through confirmation. The epistemology underneath Deutsch, who closes the list.
  3. 23The Structure of Scientific RevolutionsThomas S. Kuhn1962Science moves through paradigms that decide which questions count and which anomalies get ignored. The complication Popper never fully answered.
  4. 24The Tacit DimensionMichael Polanyi1966We can know more than we can tell. Explains why expertise resisted codification for decades, and why systems that learn from examples finally got around it.
  5. 25Individualism and Economic OrderFriedrich A. Hayek1948Contains “The Use of Knowledge in Society” (1945): prices coordinate knowledge that no single mind holds. The deepest argument for decentralized experimentation.
  6. 26Micromotives and MacrobehaviorThomas C. Schelling1978Mild individual preferences produce aggregate outcomes nobody chose, from segregated neighborhoods to tipping points. Adoption dynamics and network effects, worked out before the startup vocabulary existed.
  7. 27The Human Use of Human BeingsNorbert Wiener1950Feedback and control as the logic of machines and societies, from the founder of cybernetics, with an early warning about automation and labor.
  8. 28The Sciences of the ArtificialHerbert A. Simon1969Design, engineering, and organizations treated as one science of the artificial under bounded rationality. Simon co-wrote one of the first AI programs, and this is his clearest statement of intelligence as search and design.
IVCan markets turn ignorance into progress? 29–40
  1. 29An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of NationsAdam Smith1776Markets as a way of coordinating strangers. Worth rereading for the claim that the division of labor is limited by the extent of the market, which is an argument about scale.
  2. 30Risk, Uncertainty and ProfitFrank H. Knight1921Separates measurable risk from true uncertainty and argues that profit exists only because of the second. The theory of venture, written a century before the industry.
  3. 31The Theory of Economic DevelopmentJoseph A. Schumpeter1911The entrepreneur first appears as the agent of “new combinations” who breaks equilibrium. Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy is the famous book; this is the source.
  4. 32The Firm, the Market, and the LawRonald H. Coase1988Collects “The Nature of the Firm” (1937): firms exist because using markets has costs. Each drop in coordination cost, including the one AI is producing, moves the boundary of the firm.
  5. 33Bourgeois DignityDeirdre N. McCloskey2010The Great Enrichment followed a change in how societies talked about and honored commerce and innovation, more than any shift in capital, trade, or institutions. The middle volume of her trilogy.
  6. 34Atlas ShruggedAyn Rand1957The producer as moral hero. The novel builder culture returns to most, and worth reading against Notes from Underground twenty books earlier.
  7. 35Anarchy, State, and UtopiaRobert Nozick1974The rigorous case for a minimal state, ending in a “framework for utopia” where people sort into communities of their choosing. Network-state and charter-city arguments are versions of Part III.
  8. 36The Great TransformationKarl Polanyi1944Self-regulating markets were built by states, and society pushes back when they outrun it. Michael Polanyi’s brother, placed directly after Rand and Nozick.
  9. 37The Economy of CitiesJane Jacobs1969New work grows by adding to old work, in dense places. Also argues, against the textbook sequence, that cities came before agriculture.
  10. 38Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic PerformanceDouglass C. North1990Institutions are the rules of the game and set the incentives for everything else. The source Why Nations Fail builds on.
  11. 39The Rise and Decline of NationsMancur Olson1982Stable societies accumulate interest groups that block change, which helps explain why defeated or disrupted nations often grow fastest afterward. The most precise account of institutional sclerosis.
  12. 40Exit, Voice, and LoyaltyAlbert O. Hirschman1970Dissatisfied members can leave or speak up, and each option weakens the other. The analytic root of every exit argument later in the list.
VWhy did growth happen, and why did it slow? 41–51
  1. 41A Culture of GrowthJoel Mokyr2016A competitive market for ideas among Europe’s scholars, the Republic of Letters, set up the Industrial Revolution. Mokyr shared the 2025 Nobel for this line of work.
  2. 42Knowledge and the Wealth of NationsDavid Warsh2006The story of Paul Romer’s point that ideas are nonrival, so growth can keep compounding. The economics software and AI actually run on.
  3. 43An Essay on the Principle of PopulationThomas Robert Malthus1798Written against Condorcet and Godwin: population outruns food, so improvement is temporary. Wrong about the outcome for two centuries and still the template for every scarcity argument.
  4. 44The Ultimate Resource 2Julian L. Simon1996A revision of The Ultimate Resource (1981). Human ingenuity is the resource that does not run out, and Simon’s 1980 bet with Paul Ehrlich on commodity prices gives the Malthus rebuttal a scorecard.
  5. 45The Rational OptimistMatt Ridley2010Exchange and specialization as the engine, with ideas recombining the way genes do. The broad popular synthesis of the optimist case.
  6. 46Stubborn AttachmentsTyler Cowen2018Sustainable growth deserves near-absolute moral priority because its benefits compound across generations. The moral claim the rest of the optimist case usually leaves implicit.
  7. 47The Cost DiseaseWilliam J. Baumol2012Labor-intensive services get relatively more expensive as productivity rises elsewhere. Health care and education are the standard cases, and whether AI breaks the pattern is an open question.
  8. 48The Rise and Fall of American GrowthRobert J. Gordon2016The inventions of 1870 to 1970 (electricity, the internal combustion engine, indoor plumbing, antibiotics) changed daily life more than anything since. The strongest case that the big gains are behind us.
  9. 49Where Is My Flying Car?J. Storrs Hall2018The same question with the opposite answer: per-capita energy use flattened in the 1970s and regulation did the rest. The engineer’s account of stagnation.
  10. 50AbundanceEzra Klein and Derek Thompson2025The case for building, made from the center left: permitting, process, and a governing culture organized around veto points. Significant partly because of which coalition it comes from.
  11. 51BreakneckDan Wang2025China as an engineering state that builds and the United States as a lawyerly society that litigates, with the costs of each. Grounds the stagnation debate in a live comparison.
VIWhat is innovation made of? 52–57
  1. 52An Evolutionary Theory of Economic ChangeRichard R. Nelson and Sidney G. Winter1982Firms as bundles of routines that vary and get selected, in place of the optimizing firm of textbook economics. The foundation of evolutionary economics.
  2. 53Inside the Black BoxNathan Rosenberg1982Opens the box economists drew around technology. Science often follows engineering, and most of the value arrives through incremental improvement after the invention.
  3. 54The Nature of TechnologyW. Brian Arthur2009Technologies are assembled from other technologies and evolve by combination. The clearest account of why innovation speeds up as the stock of components grows.
  4. 55Diffusion of InnovationsEverett M. Rogers1962The S-curve and adopter categories that Crossing the Chasm and every adoption chart since are built on, drawn partly from studies of Iowa farmers adopting hybrid corn.
  5. 56Science, the Endless FrontierVannevar Bush1945The report to the president that shaped the postwar American research system and its linear model running from basic science to products.
  6. 57Pasteur’s QuadrantDonald E. Stokes1997Takes Bush’s linear model apart. Pasteur pursued fundamental understanding because he wanted to solve practical problems, and much of the most productive research sits in that quadrant.
VIITechnology meets the real world 58–70
  1. 58Networks of PowerThomas P. Hughes1983Electrification compared across Chicago, Berlin, and London, with Edison and Insull as system builders. The closest historical analog to the AI infrastructure buildout.
  2. 59The Making of the Atomic BombRichard Rhodes1986Physics converted into a weapon at wartime speed, with the science, the institutions, and the moral weight in one book. Widely read inside the frontier AI labs.
  3. 60The Idea FactoryJon Gertner2012The transistor, information theory, the laser, and the management model behind them. Also a study of what a regulated monopoly could afford to fund.
  4. 61The Dream MachineM. Mitchell Waldrop2001Licklider’s idea of human-computer symbiosis and how ARPA turned it into interactive computing and the network that became the internet. A case study in how one funder with a clear vision can redirect a field.
  5. 62Regional AdvantageAnnaLee Saxenian1994Why the Valley’s open, networked culture outcompeted Route 128’s vertically integrated firms. The ecosystem argument, made with evidence.
  6. 63The BoxMarc Levinson2006A steel box rewired global trade by cutting the cost of the whole system, in ways the early players did not foresee. The model for recognizing technologies whose impact is mostly second-order.
  7. 64Chip WarChris Miller2022How semiconductors became the strategic chokepoint, from Fairchild to TSMC to export controls. Compute as geopolitics.
  8. 65The Entrepreneurial StateMariana Mazzucato2013Public money funded the riskiest underlying science (the internet, GPS, touchscreens) before private capital commercialized it. The best version of the claim that public risk earns public returns.
  9. 66Power and ProgressDaron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson2023Gains from technology are shared only when workers and institutions can claim them, and the direction of innovation is a choice. Both authors shared the 2024 Nobel.
  10. 67Seeing Like a StateJames C. Scott1998High-modernist schemes fail because they erase the local, practical knowledge they cannot see. Hayek’s argument aimed at the state, and just as applicable to platforms.
  11. 68Normal AccidentsCharles Perrow1984In tightly coupled, interactively complex systems, some catastrophic failures are built into the design. Written after Three Mile Island and directly relevant to autonomous systems and grids.
  12. 69The Whale and the ReactorLangdon Winner1986Contains “Do Artifacts Have Politics?”: design choices settle questions of power before any law is written.
  13. 70The Collapse of Complex SocietiesJoseph A. Tainter1988Societies solve problems by adding complexity until the returns to complexity turn negative. A theory of collapse with no villain, which makes it hard to dismiss.
VIIIWho pays for the future? 71–81
  1. 71VCTom Nicholas2019The rigorous history, starting with nineteenth-century New Bedford whaling, whose return distribution looked much like venture’s.
  2. 72Doing Capitalism in the Innovation EconomyWilliam H. Janeway2012Innovation as a three-player game among the state, speculative finance, and the market economy, by an economist who spent decades investing in technology at Warburg Pincus.
  3. 73Technological Revolutions and Financial CapitalCarlota Perez2002Each technological revolution runs through an installation phase financed by a bubble, a crash, and then a deployment period. The framework most people reach for when reading the current AI capital cycle.
  4. 74Engines That Move MarketsAlasdair Nairn2002The same revolutions from the investor’s side, from railways to the internet. Most investors in transformative technologies lost money even when the technology won.
  5. 75Fortune’s FormulaWilliam Poundstone2005The Kelly criterion, from John Kelly at Bell Labs and Claude Shannon and Ed Thorp at MIT to casinos and Wall Street. Bet sizing when the edge is real and the variance is enormous.
  6. 76The Innovator’s DilemmaClayton M. Christensen1997Well-managed incumbents lose because they serve their best customers well. Widely cited and often misapplied; the original is more careful than its summaries.
  7. 777 PowersHamilton Helmer2016Seven sources of durable advantage, and a clean separation between a technology mattering and a company capturing its value.
  8. 78The Power LawSebastian Mallaby2022Venture as a distinct form of finance built around outliers, from Arthur Rock and Fairchild forward.
  9. 79High Output ManagementAndrew S. Grove1983A manager’s output is the output of the organization, and Intel’s methods for raising it.
  10. 80Zero to OnePeter Thiel with Blake Masters2014Definite optimism, secrets, and monopoly. Read here, after Girard, Knight, and Schumpeter, it reads as a compression of the tradition behind it.
  11. 81Bad BloodJohn Carreyrou2018Directly after Thiel: a company whose secret was fabricated, and the narrative, board prestige, and missing diligence that kept it going.
IXThe attempt to escape every constraint 82–95
  1. 82Daedalus, or Science and the FutureJ.B.S. Haldane1923A Cambridge lecture predicting ectogenesis and the deliberate engineering of human biology. An opening document of transhumanism and one of Huxley’s sources for Brave New World.
  2. 83The World, the Flesh and the DevilJ.D. Bernal1929A young crystallographer imagines space habitats, now called Bernal spheres, and the redesign of the human body and mind. Much of the current frontier agenda, a century early.
  3. 84The Abolition of ManC.S. Lewis1943Each new power over nature turns out to be power some people hold over others, including over future generations. Haldane later attacked Lewis’s view of science in print, and Lewis drafted a reply.
  4. 85The High FrontierGerard K. O’Neill1976A Princeton physicist’s engineering case for large rotating colonies built from lunar and asteroid material. Blue Origin’s stated long-term vision is built on it.
  5. 86The Human ConditionHannah Arendt1958Opens with Sputnik and the wish to escape the earthbound human condition, then asks what happens to action and meaning in a society of laborers without labor.
  6. 87Engines of CreationK. Eric Drexler1986Molecular manufacturing and the assembler. Much of the nanotech program has not arrived; its reasoning about exponential capability and its dangers carried straight into AI.
  7. 88The Sovereign IndividualJames Dale Davidson and William Rees-Mogg1997Predicted that digital commerce and encryption would loosen the nation-state’s hold on mobile capital and talent. Parts read as prophecy and parts as wishful thinking, and both are instructive.
  8. 89Anti-OedipusGilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari1972Capitalism as a machine that dissolves every fixed code, with a passage suggesting the way out is to go further into the process. Later accelerationists treat that passage as their starting point.

  14. 95The Scaling EraDwarkesh Patel with Gavin Leech2025The people building frontier models, in their own words, organized as a history of the scaling hypothesis. The present tense of the list.

0:00This is an intellectual genealogy of technological optimism, told in one hundred books, ordered as an argument in ten acts. It begins with the theology the idea of progress grew out of, and the psychology of the people who act on it. It moves through how knowledge grows, how markets and institutions turn knowledge into growth, how innovation is built and financed, and the attempt to engineer away every remaining constraint. About three quarters of the books make the case. The other quarter are the strongest arguments against it. Each critic sits next to the claim it attacks, and critiques of technology as such are held for the end. We begin at the beginning, with progress at its most cosmic, stated as destiny. The first book is The Phenomenon of Man, by Pierre Teilhard de Chardin, published in nineteen fifty-five.

0:56On the tenth of April, nineteen fifty-five, Easter Sunday, a seventy-three-year-old Jesuit priest died of a heart attack in a friend’s apartment in New York. Fewer than a dozen people came to the funeral. He was buried in a Jesuit cemetery in Hyde Park. Months later, in Paris, a book appeared under his name that he had been forbidden to publish while he was alive. It became a bestseller.

1:23The book was The Phenomenon of Man, and the priest was Pierre Teilhard de Chardin, pronounced tay-YAR duh shar-DAN. He had written it between nineteen thirty-eight and nineteen forty, in Beijing, during the enforced idleness of the war years. He was a paleontologist by training, a veteran of the digs at Piltdown in England and at Zhoukoudian in China, where the skull called Peking Man was found. In nineteen twenty-five, his Jesuit superiors had ordered him out of his teaching chair in Paris and forbidden him to publish anything philosophical or theological. He signed the required statement and left for China. He wrote the book knowing it could not appear in his lifetime. For years the manuscript circulated in typescript among friends and fellow Jesuits. Before his death he left his papers to a friend, and since the Church requires permission to publish only from a living writer, the book came out in nineteen fifty-five, the year he died.

2:27The argument is laid out in four parts: Before Life Came, Life, Thought, and Survival. Teilhard’s claim is that the universe is a single process, which he calls cosmogenesis, and that the direction of the process is toward greater complexity and, inseparably, greater consciousness. This is his law of complexity-consciousness. As matter arranges itself into more complex and more centered forms, its inner life, its psychic dimension, intensifies in step. He divides the energy in each particle into two components. Tangential energy is the energy of connection with other things of the same order. Radial energy is the energy of inward arrangement, of centering, and it is radial energy that grows along the axis of complexity. He concedes that this radial energy is not conserved in the physicist’s sense.

3:18The three phases of the single trajectory get his own names. Geogenesis, the evolution of the earth and of matter before life. Biogenesis, the evolution of life. Noogenesis, the evolution of thought. With the arrival of reflective human minds, he says, a new planetary envelope forms above the biosphere: the noosphere, the thinking layer, a sphere of shared thought, culture, and communication, in which humanity converges into what he calls a single interthinking group. And the trajectory does not stop there. The noosphere intensifies toward a final center of convergence, a supreme personal consciousness he calls the Omega Point, which he identifies with the cosmic Christ of Saint Paul’s letters, that God may be all in all. The book’s epilogue is titled The Christian Phenomenon, and it leaves no doubt that the Omega Point is an eschatological claim, a claim about the end and purpose of history.

4:17In a foreword written in nineteen forty-seven, Teilhard insists on how the book should be read. In his words, it must be read not as metaphysics, still less as theology, but purely and simply as a scientific treatise. That sentence is the hinge on which the whole reception turns.

4:37The English translation, by Bernard Wall, appeared in nineteen fifty-nine with an introduction by the evolutionary biologist Julian Huxley. Huxley calls it a very remarkable work by a very remarkable human being, and then spends much of the introduction disagreeing with it. He cannot follow the Omega Point, and he warns against personifying what is not personal. In nineteen sixty-one, the biologist Peter Medawar reviewed the book in the philosophy journal Mind, and the review remains the most famous demolition in modern science writing. He called it a bag of tricks. He wrote that the greater part of it was nonsense, tricked out with metaphysical conceits, composed in an all but totally unintelligible style, which readers mistook for evidence of profundity. The paleontologist George Gaylord Simpson made the colder charge, in This View of Life in nineteen sixty-four: that Teilhard’s premises were religious, and he discovered nothing in evolution that he had not already presupposed. And in nineteen sixty-two, the Holy Office, with the approval of Pope John the Twenty-Third, issued a monitum, a formal warning, against Teilhard’s works, saying they abounded in ambiguities and serious errors offensive to Catholic doctrine, and urging bishops and seminary rectors to protect the young from them. The works were never placed on the Index of Forbidden Books, and the warning has never been formally rescinded.

6:06So the book was attacked from both sides at once: by scientists who said it was theology pretending to be science, and by the Church, which said its theology was unsound. So much for what the book’s first readers could know. The rest is hindsight.

6:24The strongest objection is Simpson’s, and it deserves to be stated at full strength. If you assume at the outset that the universe is converging on a divine center, then every increase in complexity you observe will look like evidence for the convergence. The conclusion is loaded into the premises. Teilhard’s radial energy has no physical correlate. No experiment could distinguish a universe with radial energy from one without it. And evolutionary biology after him rejected the very idea his system needs: orthogenesis, the doctrine that evolution has a built-in direction. Jacques Monod, in Chance and Necessity in nineteen seventy, called directional evolution a form of animism, a projection of the human mind’s own purposefulness onto nature. The consensus since is that natural selection works without any built-in goal.

7:15There is also the matter of the prose. Medawar’s complaint was not only about the argument. He thought the obscurity was doing argumentative work, that sentences which cannot be parsed cannot be refuted, and that the book’s atmosphere of profundity was manufactured by its style. Defenders reply that Teilhard was translating between science and theology, and that the difficulty is real, not decorative. The dispute is unresolvable, which is itself a data point.

7:44Then the afterlife. The word noosphere escaped the book and took on a life of its own. In nineteen ninety-five, Wired magazine declared that Teilhard had seen the Net coming half a century early, the globe clothing itself with a brain. The internet-as-noosphere reading is now the common one, though it keeps the thinking layer and drops the teleology, which is to say it keeps the image and drops the argument. The physicist Frank Tipler borrowed the Omega Point for The Physics of Immortality in nineteen ninety-four, but his version is a physical cosmology, a closed universe ending in a singularity where a universal computer resurrects the dead by emulation. He judged Teilhard’s physics completely wrong. The transhumanists claimed him too. The philosopher Eric Steinhart has argued that Teilhard was among the first to describe an acceleration of intelligence toward something like a singularity, and Ray Kurzweil has quoted his line that evolution moves toward our conception of God.

8:48And Rome came around, partway. No pope has lifted the nineteen sixty-two warning, but Pope Benedict the Sixteenth quoted Teilhard approvingly in two thousand nine, and Pope Francis cited him in Laudato Si in two thousand fifteen, the first mention of Teilhard in a papal encyclical. In two thousand twenty-five, for the seventieth anniversary of his death, the Vatican newspaper ran six articles on him.

9:14Two darker threads belong in any honest account, and both lie mostly outside the book itself. The scholar John Slattery has argued, in peer-reviewed work, that Teilhard’s letters and essays from the nineteen twenties to his death contain eugenic and racially hierarchical views, including praise for eugenic programs and dissent from UNESCO’s statement on the equality of races. His defenders, including the biographer Claude Cuenot, reply that these were private, speculative passages reflecting the racial science of the day, and that Slattery builds too much on too few citations. The dispute is live. On Piltdown: Stephen Jay Gould suggested in nineteen eighty that Teilhard, who found a canine tooth at the Piltdown site in nineteen thirteen, may have known the fossils were a hoax that began as a joke. A two thousand sixteen study in Royal Society Open Science, using DNA and spectroscopy, concluded the forger was Charles Dawson, acting alone. No direct evidence implicates Teilhard.

10:16One last verified fact. The most famous sentence attributed to Teilhard, that we are not human beings having a spiritual experience but spiritual beings having a human experience, is not his. Researchers have traced it to the self-help writer Wayne Dyer, in a nineteen eighty-eight advertisement. It entered circulation as Teilhard’s through a citation-free attribution in a business bestseller. The noosphere, it turns out, misremembers.

10:42He died on Easter Sunday, as he once said he wished to, and was buried with fewer than a dozen mourners. Seventy years later, the Vatican’s own newspaper devoted six articles to him, and the warning against his books has never been lifted. Both things are true.
