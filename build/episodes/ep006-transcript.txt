He had fled Germany fifteen years earlier, taught in Rome and at Tohoku University in Sendai, Japan, and arrived in America in nineteen forty-one to teach at Hartford Seminary. The question the war had made unavoidable was his subject: whether history means anything at all.

[0:31] This is A Genealogy of Technological Optimism, episode six of one hundred. We're in Act One: Why should history go anywhere?

[0:42] Nisbet's open question was whether the modern idea of progress still needs its Christian maker. Time with a direction, a future worth waiting for. Those are older parts. Karl Löwith, pronounced LURR-vit, said yes, they do. He was fifty-two, a philosopher trained by Husserl and Heidegger at Freiburg, a Protestant of Jewish descent who had watched his teacher join the Nazi party in nineteen thirty-three and never forgave him. He later called Heidegger a godless theologian. The book was called Meaning in History, and its argument is simple to state and hard to shake. Every modern philosophy of history is Christian eschatology with the theology taken out. As the scholar Daniel Herskowitz quotes him, it is as Christian by derivation as it is non-Christian by consequence.

[1:34] The machinery runs on a three-way contrast. The Greeks, in Löwith's telling, saw time as a circle. Things recur. There is no direction to discover, so there is no philosophy of history to write. The question of where history is going could not arise. The biblical tradition drew a line instead: creation to consummation, a beginning, a middle, and an end guaranteed from outside time. Eschatology is the doctrine of those last things. Then comes the modern hybrid, and this is the charge. It keeps the Christian form, a story moving toward fulfillment, and drops creation and consummation. It keeps the Greek endlessness and drops the circle. The result is progress: forward motion with no terminus, a religion of the future preached by people who stopped believing in the religion.

[2:30] The book walks backward to make the case. It starts in the nineteenth century with Burckhardt, moves through Marx and Hegel, through Proudhon, Comte, Condorcet and Turgot, then back through Voltaire, Vico, Bossuet, Joachim of Fiore, Augustine, Orosius, and the Bible itself. The first chapter treats Jacob Burckhardt, the subject of Löwith's book of nineteen thirty-six, and the last appendix treats Nietzsche's eternal recurrence, the subject of his book of nineteen thirty-five. The genealogy opens and closes with the two men he knew best. And he spares the theologians nothing. He is skeptical of every attempt to read God's hand in the events, not only the secular ones. An appendix follows Joachim's pattern into the present, under the title Modern Transfigurations of Joachism.

[3:25] Joachim of Fiore. Nisbet placed the monk's visions of an earthly paradise at the root of the idea of progress, and Löwith gives him a full chapter. Joachim divided history into three ages, the age of the Father, the age of the Son, and a coming age of the Spirit. Löwith's point is that every three-stage theory of history after him, including the secular ones, is running Joachim's pattern. The section on Condorcet and Turgot takes the tenth epoch, the guaranteed future with its three hopes, as Exhibit A. Joachim's third age returns as Condorcet's tenth epoch, minus the Spirit.

[4:06] The line everyone quotes comes near the very end. As Herskowitz quotes him, Löwith wrote that the modern mind has not made up its mind whether it should be Christian or pagan. It sees with one eye of faith and one of reason. Hence its vision is necessarily dim in comparison with either Greek or biblical thinking. It is one of the book's last sentences, on page two hundred and seven of the Chicago edition.

[4:33] The first readers took the book seriously, and the objections started immediately. Helmut Kuhn, reviewing it in the Journal of Philosophy in December of nineteen forty-nine, called it a book of distinction and great importance, and its author a master of philosophical interpretation. Kuhn also put the lasting objection on the table in the same review. As he wrote, Löwith too constructs a philosophical history towards an eschaton, and in a peculiarly inverted manner he is among the believers in progress, with the goal being the undoing of the things done. A genealogy that total becomes its own philosophy of history, marching toward its own ending. Carl Schmitt's nineteen fifty essay, Three Possibilities for a Christian Conception of History, followed Löwith's path from philosophy of history to eschatology and agreed with the diagnosis. The Enlightenment's belief in progress was secularized Judaism and Christianity.

[5:35] The Conclusion refuses the cure, showing that dependence leaves the problem of history harder than before. We are not closer to an answer. His Conclusion opens, as the study "Genealogies of the Secular" quotes him, with a flat sentence. Man's historical experience is one of steady failure. The Epilogue ends by asking whether the future is really the proper horizon of a human life. He will not hand you a better philosophy of history, and he will not hand you back the theology either.

[6:07] The deeper answer took seventeen years. Hans Blumenberg's came in nineteen sixty-six, first sketched at a philosophy congress in Münster in nineteen sixty-two. There he attacked the secularization story at its root. His argument, stated fairly, is that what looks like stolen theology is new thought moving into problems the old theology left behind. Progress anticipates a development inside history. Eschatology waits for an irruption from beyond it. Those are not the same shape, and calling one the secularization of the other proves nothing about where the substance came from. What continues across the break, he argued, is not substances but problems. Blumenberg gets the next episode.

[6:51] Löwith himself narrowed the claim. In a nineteen sixty-eight review of Blumenberg's book, he said he had never meant theft in the legal sense. Applied to historical epochs, he wrote, there can be no question of legitimacy or illegitimacy. What he meant was subtler. Old Testament prophecy and Christian eschatology, in his restated words, created a horizon of the future and of a future fulfillment. That horizon made the modern concept of history possible.

[7:20] I think the narrowed claim is the one that stands. The biblical tradition gave the modern mind its questions, even if nothing was stolen. Condorcet's tenth epoch promises a fulfilled future with no God to deliver it. The shape is still there. The eschatological structure survives the loss of its theology. But Kuhn's charge stands too. A book that buries every philosophy of history is itself marching somewhere. You can hear it in how AI labs describe the road to superintelligence, with the certainty the old eschatologists reserved for the last things.
