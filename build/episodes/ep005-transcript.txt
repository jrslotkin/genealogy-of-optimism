[0:00] In July of nineteen seventy-nine, Jimmy Carter told the country its crisis was one of confidence. He never used the word malaise. That winter, a sixty-six-year-old sociologist at a Washington think tank published a history with a startling claim. The idea of progress was twenty-five hundred years old, far older than the Enlightenment usually credited with inventing it.

[0:26] This is A Genealogy of Technological Optimism, episode five of one hundred. We're in Act One: Why should history go anywhere?

[0:35] The sociologist was Robert Nisbet, pronounced NIZ-bit. He was born in nineteen thirteen in Los Angeles and raised in Maricopa, California, where his father managed a lumber yard. He took his doctorate at Berkeley in nineteen thirty-nine, founded the sociology department there, served in the Army on Saipan during the war, and left Berkeley in nineteen fifty-three for a career that ended in the Albert Schweitzer chair at Columbia. He retired in nineteen seventy-eight and wrote the book at the American Enterprise Institute. His name had been made by The Quest for Community, published in nineteen fifty-three, which argued that the modern state had eaten the institutions standing between the person and the government: the family, the church, the guild.

[1:24] The new book was called History of the Idea of Progress, and it was aimed at the standard story. That story was J. B. Bury's The Idea of Progress, published in nineteen twenty. Bury's rule was strict. You don't have the idea of Progress, capital P, until you believe civilization is destined to advance indefinitely into the future. Everything before the seventeenth century, he said, was just people noticing that knowledge grows. Nisbet thought that rule rigged the game. His counterclaim runs the whole book. The idea is one continuous Western tradition, twenty-five hundred years long, and it is religious at the root.

[2:07] His definition is plain. The idea of progress holds that mankind has advanced from primitive beginnings, is advancing now, and will keep advancing through the foreseeable future. And it has to feel like physics. As he puts it, advance from the inferior to the superior must seem as real and certain as anything in the laws of nature.

[2:28] The evidence marches in order. The classical world, from Hesiod through Seneca, on the growth of knowledge and the arts. The early Christians, Augustine above all, who took the Jewish story of a directed history and stretched it over the whole human race. The Middle Ages, with Joachim of Fiore's visions of an earthly paradise and Dante's plan for a universal monarchy. Then a surprise Nisbet admits himself. The Renaissance, where the idea goes into total eclipse, because the humanists broke with the thousand years behind them and fell back on cycles. Then the Great Renewal. The Reformation, Puritan millenarianism, and the long zenith from seventeen fifty to nineteen hundred, when progress became, in his phrase, virtually a universal religion on both sides of the Atlantic.

[3:22] The boldest move is the sorting. The triumph gets two chapters, Progress as Freedom and Progress as Power. In the freedom camp, from Turgot through Adam Smith to Herbert Spencer, progress means the individual getting out from under the state. Condorcet is filed there too, one name in a very long roll call. Malthus again too, which surprises anyone who met him in the last episode as the man who answered Condorcet with arithmetic. Nisbet counts him a believer. In the power camp, from Rousseau through Saint-Simon, Hegel and Marx, with Comte, progress means the collective accumulating power.

[4:04] The last chapters turn from history to the present. The Persistence of Progress, then Progress at Bay. Nisbet lists five premises the idea stands on: belief in the value of the past, conviction of the nobility of Western civilization, acceptance of economic and technological growth, faith in reason and science, and belief in the worth of life on this earth. He says the premises are going, and the idea is going with them. The epilogue's prescription is the book's strangest passage. Only a religious renewal can restore the faith. And Teilhard de Chardin, the Jesuit paleontologist from Episode One, gets four pages in The Persistence of Progress. Nisbet names him the twentieth century's philosopher of history, the central figure of its cosmic myth.

[4:57] Most of the early reviews were hostile. J. M. Cameron, in the New York Review of Books in April of nineteen eighty, called it a scissors-and-paste job, and a dull one. But Robert Solomon, in the Chicago Review that year, praised the book while turning its own evidence against it. The unitary faith Nisbet defends, Solomon argued, has an insidious side, a history of condoning slavery and serving racism and colonialism. And Robert Andelson, in Modern Age in the winter of nineteen eighty-one, put the lasting objection on the table. The book never settles what the idea is. The vital elements listed near the start don't match the five premises listed near the end.

[5:40] The definition is the first thing to wobble. If Hesiod, Augustine, Malthus and Spencer all believed in progress, the word may be covering too much to explain anything. Robert Andelson drew the sharpest line, borrowing Russell Kirk's warning from nineteen fifty-three that belief in the idea of progress is not the same thing as believing in Providence, and charging that Nisbet, for all his conservatism, had blurred the two. Gorman Beauchamp, quoting the historian Warren Wagar, said there is no single gold-plated Idea of Progress to measure the rest against. There are many ideas of progress, and the lines between them are hard to draw.

[6:22] Bury's test still bites. Noticing that knowledge grows is not the same as believing history is destined to keep improving. Frank Manuel, the historian of utopias, dated the full-blown theory to late eighteenth-century France, really the nineteenth. Nisbet's own Renaissance eclipse admits the continuity breaks. And Gorman Beauchamp, reviewing the book in nineteen eighty-two, said the roll call is a paperchase of great names that never shows what ordinary people believed. We don't know what most people thought about progress for most of that history.

[6:58] And the book contradicts itself. The creed says advance is as certain as the laws of nature. The last chapter says civilization may not survive the loss of the faith. Beauchamp put it dryly. A creed of historical necessity has nothing to fear. And Beauchamp noted a telling absence. A history of the West on this scale finds no room for the Holocaust.

[7:25] Read today, the epilogue's warnings look wrong. The ominous retreat from reason that Nisbet reported never quite arrived. The decades after nineteen eighty brought the personal computer, the internet, the genome. Steven Pinker answered the declinists with seventy-five charts.
