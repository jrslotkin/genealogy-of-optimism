0:00This is an intellectual genealogy of technological optimism, told in one hundred books, ordered as an argument in ten acts. It begins with the theology the idea of progress grew out of, and the psychology of the people who act on it. It moves through how knowledge grows, how markets and institutions turn knowledge into growth, how innovation is built and financed, and the attempt to engineer away every remaining constraint. About three quarters of the books make the case. The other quarter are the strongest arguments against it. Each critic sits next to the claim it attacks, and critiques of technology as such are held for the end. We begin at the beginning, with progress at its most cosmic, stated as destiny. The first book is The Phenomenon of Man, by Pierre Teilhard de Chardin, published in nineteen fifty-five.

0:54On the tenth of April, nineteen fifty-five, Easter Sunday, a seventy-three-year-old Jesuit priest died of a heart attack in a friend’s apartment in New York. Fewer than a dozen people came to the funeral. He was buried in a Jesuit cemetery in Hyde Park. Months later, in Paris, a book appeared under his name that he had been forbidden to publish while he was alive. It became a bestseller.

1:20The book was The Phenomenon of Man, and the priest was Pierre Teilhard de Chardin, pronounced tay-YAR duh shar-DAN. He had written it between nineteen thirty-eight and nineteen forty, in Beijing, during the enforced idleness of the war years. He was a paleontologist by training, a veteran of the digs at Piltdown in England and at Zhoukoudian in China, where the skull called Peking Man was found. In nineteen twenty-five, his Jesuit superiors had ordered him out of his teaching chair in Paris and forbidden him to publish anything philosophical or theological. He signed the required statement and left for China. He wrote the book knowing it could not appear in his lifetime. For years the manuscript circulated in typescript among friends and fellow Jesuits. Before his death he left his papers to a friend, and since the Church requires permission to publish only from a living writer, the book came out in nineteen fifty-five, the year he died.

2:23The argument is laid out in four parts: Before Life Came, Life, Thought, and Survival. Teilhard’s claim is that the universe is a single process, which he calls cosmogenesis, and that the direction of the process is toward greater complexity and, inseparably, greater consciousness. This is his law of complexity-consciousness. As matter arranges itself into more complex and more centered forms, its inner life, its psychic dimension, intensifies in step. He divides the energy in each particle into two components. Tangential energy is the energy of connection with other things of the same order. Radial energy is the energy of inward arrangement, of centering, and it is radial energy that grows along the axis of complexity. He concedes that this radial energy is not conserved in the physicist’s sense.

3:22The three phases of the single trajectory get his own names. Geogenesis, the evolution of the earth and of matter before life. Biogenesis, the evolution of life. Noogenesis, the evolution of thought. With the arrival of reflective human minds, he says, a new planetary envelope forms above the biosphere: the noosphere, the thinking layer, a sphere of shared thought, culture, and communication, in which humanity converges into what he calls a single interthinking group. And the trajectory does not stop there. The noosphere intensifies toward a final center of convergence, a supreme personal consciousness he calls the Omega Point, which he identifies with the cosmic Christ of Saint Paul’s letters, that God may be all in all. The book’s epilogue is titled The Christian Phenomenon, and it leaves no doubt that the Omega Point is an eschatological claim, a claim about the end and purpose of history.

4:28In a foreword written in nineteen forty-seven, Teilhard insists on how the book should be read. In his words, it must be read not as metaphysics, still less as theology, but purely and simply as a scientific treatise. That sentence is the hinge on which the whole reception turns.

4:47The English translation, by Bernard Wall, appeared in nineteen fifty-nine with an introduction by the evolutionary biologist Julian Huxley. Huxley calls it a very remarkable work by a very remarkable human being, and then spends much of the introduction disagreeing with it. He cannot follow the Omega Point, and he warns against personifying what is not personal. In nineteen sixty-one, the biologist Peter Medawar reviewed the book in the philosophy journal Mind, and the review remains the most famous demolition in modern science writing. He called it a bag of tricks. He wrote that the greater part of it was nonsense, tricked out with metaphysical conceits, composed in an all but totally unintelligible style, which readers mistook for evidence of profundity. The paleontologist George Gaylord Simpson made the colder charge, in This View of Life in nineteen sixty-four: that Teilhard’s premises were religious, and he discovered nothing in evolution that he had not already presupposed. And in nineteen sixty-two, the Holy Office, with the approval of Pope John the Twenty-Third, issued a monitum, a formal warning, against Teilhard’s works, saying they abounded in ambiguities and serious errors offensive to Catholic doctrine, and urging bishops and seminary rectors to protect the young from them. The works were never placed on the Index of Forbidden Books, and the warning has never been formally rescinded.

6:16So the book was attacked from both sides at once: by scientists who said it was theology pretending to be science, and by the Church, which said its theology was unsound. So much for what the book’s first readers could know. The rest is hindsight.

6:31The strongest objection is Simpson’s, and it deserves to be stated at full strength. If you assume at the outset that the universe is converging on a divine center, then every increase in complexity you observe will look like evidence for the convergence. The conclusion is loaded into the premises. Teilhard’s radial energy has no physical correlate. No experiment could distinguish a universe with radial energy from one without it. And evolutionary biology after him rejected the very idea his system needs: orthogenesis, the doctrine that evolution has a built-in direction. Jacques Monod, in Chance and Necessity in nineteen seventy, called directional evolution a form of animism, a projection of the human mind’s own purposefulness onto nature. The consensus since is that natural selection works without any built-in goal.

7:28There is also the matter of the prose. Medawar’s complaint was not only about the argument. He thought the obscurity was doing argumentative work, that sentences which cannot be parsed cannot be refuted, and that the book’s atmosphere of profundity was manufactured by its style. Defenders reply that Teilhard was translating between science and theology, and that the difficulty is real, not decorative. The dispute is unresolvable, which is itself a data point.

7:59Then the afterlife. The word noosphere escaped the book and took on a life of its own. In nineteen ninety-five, Wired magazine declared that Teilhard had seen the Net coming half a century early, the globe clothing itself with a brain. The internet-as-noosphere reading is now the common one, though it keeps the thinking layer and drops the teleology, which is to say it keeps the image and drops the argument. The physicist Frank Tipler borrowed the Omega Point for The Physics of Immortality in nineteen ninety-four, but his version is a physical cosmology, a closed universe ending in a singularity where a universal computer resurrects the dead by emulation. He judged Teilhard’s physics completely wrong. The transhumanists claimed him too. The philosopher Eric Steinhart has argued that Teilhard was among the first to describe an acceleration of intelligence toward something like a singularity, and Ray Kurzweil has quoted his line that evolution moves toward our conception of God.

8:59And Rome came around, partway. No pope has lifted the nineteen sixty-two warning, but Pope Benedict the Sixteenth quoted Teilhard approvingly in two thousand nine, and Pope Francis cited him in Laudato Si in two thousand fifteen, the first mention of Teilhard in a papal encyclical. In two thousand twenty-five, for the seventieth anniversary of his death, the Vatican newspaper ran six articles on him.

9:31Two darker threads belong in any honest account, and both lie mostly outside the book itself. The scholar John Slattery has argued, in peer-reviewed work, that Teilhard’s letters and essays from the nineteen twenties to his death contain eugenic and racially hierarchical views, including praise for eugenic programs and dissent from UNESCO’s statement on the equality of races. His defenders, including the biographer Claude Cuenot, reply that these were private, speculative passages reflecting the racial science of the day, and that Slattery builds too much on too few citations. The dispute is live. On Piltdown: Stephen Jay Gould suggested in nineteen eighty that Teilhard, who found a canine tooth at the Piltdown site in nineteen thirteen, may have known the fossils were a hoax that began as a joke. A two thousand sixteen study in Royal Society Open Science, using DNA and spectroscopy, concluded the forger was Charles Dawson, acting alone. No direct evidence implicates Teilhard.

10:42One last verified fact. The most famous sentence attributed to Teilhard, that we are not human beings having a spiritual experience but spiritual beings having a human experience, is not his. Researchers have traced it to the self-help writer Wayne Dyer, in a nineteen eighty-eight advertisement. It entered circulation as Teilhard’s through a citation-free attribution in a business bestseller. The noosphere, it turns out, misremembers.

11:10He died on Easter Sunday, as he once said he wished to, and was buried with fewer than a dozen mourners. Seventy years later, the Vatican’s own newspaper devoted six articles to him, and the warning against his books has never been lifted. Both things are true.
