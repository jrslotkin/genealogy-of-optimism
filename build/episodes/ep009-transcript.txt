There's a democratic polity, where political power is limited and divided. There's an economy based on markets and incentives. And there's a moral and cultural system, pluralistic and liberal in the broad sense, carried by families, churches, schools, and the press. His claim is that the three need each other. Political democracy, he argues, can only be sustained alongside a market economy, and both rest on habits they can't produce by themselves, habits like self-restraint and trust. [2:53] What he's defending is narrower than the capitalism his critics usually attack. A market economy on its own isn't his subject. In his account the moral and cultural system has to tame and correct commerce from outside commerce, and the political system has to be kept from swallowing the other two. At the center of a pluralist order he places an empty shrine. No church, party, or state gets to install its symbol there, because no single account of what people seek there is adequate for all of them. The separation does theological work for him. He reads the Trinity and the Incarnation as warrants for pluralism and for taking material life seriously, and he treats original sin as a design constraint.
