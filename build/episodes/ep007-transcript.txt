[0:00] In the spring of nineteen thirty-nine, at the Katharineum school in Lübeck, a schoolboy sat his final examinations and took the highest mark in his year. Within the year he'd learned that Germany's universities were closed to him anyway. The state counted him a half-Jew, through his mother, and the certificate in his hand carried less weight than the classification.

[0:23] This is A Genealogy of Technological Optimism, episode seven of one hundred. We're in Act One: Why should history go anywhere?

[0:32] The schoolboy was Hans Blumenberg. He studied where he was allowed, at Catholic colleges in Paderborn and Frankfurt, until those doors closed too. Then he worked at the Dräger factory in Lübeck, which made gas masks and gear for submarines, and the job shielded him for a time. In February of nineteen forty-five he was seized and sent to a labor camp at Zerbst, run by the Organisation Todt. Heinrich Dräger, who ran the firm, had him reclaimed. He went back to Lübeck and hid, partly with his future wife's family, until the war ended. That fall he enrolled at Hamburg. He took his doctorate at Kiel in nineteen forty-seven, under Ludwig Landgrebe, a former assistant to Husserl, and completed his habilitation there in nineteen fifty. Chairs followed at Giessen in nineteen sixty and Bochum in nineteen sixty-five. The book that made his name appeared in Frankfurt in nineteen sixty-six.

[1:35] The book is Blumenberg's answer to an accusation. Löwith again: in nineteen forty-nine he'd argued that our belief in progress descends from Christian eschatology, the doctrine of last things, with God removed and the expectation kept. Carl Schmitt, the legal theorist, had made a matching claim for politics in nineteen twenty-two, that the central concepts of the modern state are theological concepts in secular dress. Put the two together and modernity stands accused of living on stolen property. Blumenberg heard the accusation as a lawyer would. The first part of his book is a critique of secularization as a category of historical wrong, and the word legitimacy in his title answers in the same legal register.

[2:25] His counter starts with a distinction between questions and answers. A question can outlive every answer given to it. When the theological answers lost their hold, the questions they'd organized didn't retire with them. They stood open, and new occupants moved in. Blumenberg's word for this is reoccupation. What gets inherited is the vacancy, the place in our thinking where an answer has to sit. What fills the place can be, and in his telling is, genuinely new. Apply that to progress and the shape changes. An eschaton arrives from outside the story and ends it. Judgment is rendered and the account closes. Progress never arrives. People build it, slowly, from inside history, with methods they can check, and every result sets the next task. Blumenberg traces the shape of it to two early modern experiences, organized science outgrowing the authority of Aristotle, and the quarrel in literature over whether the ancients could be surpassed.

[3:30] The book's second part explains why the vacancy opened at all, and it starts with theological absolutism. Late medieval theology, above all the nominalists around William of Ockham, exalted God's absolute power until the world stopped making sense. A God who could will anything, whose decrees gave no reasons we could follow, left creation looking arbitrary and faintly hostile. Blumenberg reaches for an older name for that hostility. Gnosticism, the ancient teaching that the visible world is a botched or malicious creation, was supposed to have been defeated by Augustine's church. He argues it slipped back in wearing scholastic dress, through the hidden and unaccountable God. Self-assertion is the modern reply. He means something nearer self-defense than self-worship. Faced with a cosmos that couldn't be trusted, people began securing the conditions of their lives with knowledge they could test. The modern age, on his account, stands because it answered a real emergency with means of its own devising.

[4:38] The third part puts a single appetite on trial. Theoretical curiosity, the wish to know things because they're there to be known, had been entered in the catalog of vices, and Augustine's name carries much of the blame in Blumenberg's telling. The trial runs for centuries in his pages, through medieval schoolmen worrying the charge, until the verdict reverses and curiosity becomes an engine of science. The fourth part stations two figures at the threshold. Nicholas of Cusa, the fifteenth-century cardinal whose speculations loosened the closed medieval cosmos, and Giordano Bruno, the Nolan, who took the infinite universe literally. Bruno was burned at Rome in sixteen hundred, and Blumenberg dwells on the moment he turned his face away from the crucifix offered to him at the stake. We have that gesture from a single witness, Kaspar Schoppe, who described it in a letter written the same day. Blumenberg treats the scene as a threshold you can see.

[5:44] Löwith read the book as an attack aimed at him, and in a measure it was. His review came in nineteen sixty-eight. He hadn't meant theft in any legal sense, he said. He'd meant that two expectations can share a posture, the habit of living in hope toward a fulfillment waiting in the future. That winter the two men exchanged letters, before the review appeared, and the positions didn't move. The revised edition, enlarged, appeared in parts between nineteen seventy-three and nineteen seventy-six. English readers waited longer. Wallace's translation came out in nineteen eighty-three, a book of six hundred and seventy-seven pages. Martin Jay's long review in History and Theory followed in nineteen eighty-five. By then the argument had been running in German for nineteen years.

[6:37] Löwith's reply has force. If the modern hope and the Christian hope share a posture toward the future, then calling the answers new may settle less than the book claims. Robert Pippin, the Chicago philosopher, pressed a version of this point. The differences in shape between an ending that arrives and a task that doesn't, he argued, don't by themselves break an analogy, and analogies were all Löwith ever needed. The machinery also has a gap in it. Reoccupation explains how a new answer takes an empty seat. It says much less about where the new answer came from, and Ulrike Kistner treats that silence as the theory's soft spot. And the Augustine of the curiosity chapters has drawn fire from Augustine's own readers. Joseph Rivera, a theologian at Dublin City University, argued in twenty twenty-one that Blumenberg assimilates Augustine to the Gnostics too quickly, and that the suspicion of curiosity in Augustine's writing has more give in it than the vice charge allows.

[7:44] I think Blumenberg wins the case he actually argued. He shows the modern answers are genuinely new, and a new answer doesn't become an old doctrine because it addresses an old question. Löwith's smaller claim survives the verdict. Blumenberg's machinery needs the old questions to stay standing, and a history of the questions is a kind of descent Löwith can claim. Where I part from Blumenberg is the courtroom itself. Legitimacy is a lawyer's test, and an age can pass it and still misjudge what its tools are doing. You hear the older accusation now in a different key, when a technology roadmap gets presented in the cadence of destiny and the cadence gets treated as evidence that the future is owed. The debate didn't end in nineteen sixty-eight. It changed its burden of proof. Michael Allen Gillespie's The Theological Origins of Modernity, published in two thousand eight, builds its account of modernity's origins as an argument with Blumenberg.
