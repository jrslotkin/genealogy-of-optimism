[0:00] In the reading room of Moscow's Rumyantsev Museum, late in the nineteenth century, there was a librarian whom strangers mistook for a beggar. His coat was a rag. He ate hard rolls and drank tea, gave away his salary to students, and walked everywhere, even in winter. He had read everything, and he believed every book in the building was evidence for a single charge against humanity: that we have accepted death.

[0:29] This is A Genealogy of Technological Optimism, episode two of one hundred. We're in Act One: Why should history go anywhere?

[0:37] His name was Nikolai Fedorov, born in eighteen twenty-nine, the illegitimate son of a Russian prince and a peasant woman. He taught in provincial schools and became an assistant librarian, where he stayed for twenty-five years. Readers received not only the books they requested but everything else he thought necessary. The young Konstantin Tsiolkovsky studied under his direction for three years. Tolstoy admired his asceticism. Dostoevsky praised his logic. He was known, to those who knew him, as the Socrates of Moscow. He published almost nothing in his lifetime, partly from opposition to copyright, and partly because he saw writing as a prelude to action. When he died in nineteen oh three, two disciples gathered his notes, drafts, and essays into two volumes. The first appeared in nineteen oh six under the title The Philosophy of the Common Task.

[1:33] The claim is as stark as any in philosophy. Death, Fedorov argues, is not a fact to be accepted or a mystery to be contemplated. It is the single root of every human problem, and every solution that leaves it untouched is a fraud. War, poverty, disease, the whole catalogue of suffering: all of it flows from mortality, and all of it ends when mortality ends. The task he sets is the physical resurrection of everyone who has ever lived, achieved not by God at the end of time but by human beings, through science. This is what he means by the common task, and it is not a metaphor. In a letter to one of Fedorov's followers in eighteen seventy-eight, Dostoevsky asked the question: is this meant literally, will the dead be raised, quote, not only in our minds, not allegorically, but in fact, in person, actually, in bodies. The answer, from Fedorov's writings, is an emphatic yes.

[2:36] To understand what he is proposing, you need his moral psychology. We receive life from our fathers, Fedorov says, and we repay the debt by forgetting them. Modern life is built on what he calls non-kindred relations: the living alienated from each other, and all of the living alienated from the dead. Progress itself, in his account, is an accusation. It makes ancestors into the accused and descendants into judges, sacrificing the fathers for the comfort of the sons. Against this he sets sonship as well as brotherhood: we owe our existence to those who came before, and the debt can only be repaid by giving life back. Christianity, in his reading, is not a set of beliefs about the afterlife. It is a project. Christ showed what was to be done, in the raising of Lazarus, but not how. The how is ours to discover. This is why he called his philosophy supramoralism: morality raised to the level of a cosmic engineering project. The museum becomes in his writing the central institution of the future, because the museum keeps humanity's memory of the dead, and memory is the first step toward restoration.

[3:54] The mechanics are where the philosophy turns strange, and prophetic. Death is disintegration, the scattering of the particles that made up a person. But those particles cannot leave finite space, so they can in principle be found, gathered, and reassembled. The catch is that the Earth is too small a workshop. To recover the dust of the ancestors, Fedorov writes, research teams will have to travel to the moon, the planets, and the distant points of the universe, and the resurrected will need those worlds to live on, their bodies perhaps rebuilt to survive conditions no human body could now endure. Space travel is therefore not an adventure but a moral necessity, built into the logic of the project. Along the way he sketches a program that reads like a checklist from a later century: regulation of the weather, solar energy in place of fossil fuels, the Earth managed as an ecosystem rather than exploited as a mine, and failing human organs replaced with artificial ones.

[5:00] In his own lifetime almost no one read him, because there was almost nothing to read. His influence traveled by conversation, in that museum reading room. Dostoevsky wrote in his Writer's Diary that he had rarely read anything more logical. The philosopher Vladimir Solovyov wrote to Fedorov in eighteen eighty-one that he accepted the project unconditionally, calling him teacher and spiritual father. Tolstoy, who could never live up to his own ascetic ideals, revered a man who did. After Fedorov's death the disciples' volumes found a real audience. Fedorovism became a force in the intellectual ferment of pre-revolutionary Russia and the early Soviet years. Stalin's purges of the nineteen thirties suppressed it, and in the West it barely registered. Frederick Copleston's four-hundred-and-forty-five-page history of Russian philosophy mentions Fedorov twice, both times in footnotes.

[5:53] Now the turn, judged first by what Fedorov and his first readers could know. The strongest objection available in his own century was theological, put most sharply by the Orthodox theologian Sergei Bulgakov. In Christian doctrine, resurrection is God's work, promised at the end of time. Fedorov makes it man's work, scheduled for the foreseeable future. Bulgakov's charge is that the project never answers its own first question: what is life, and what is death. If a person is only matter, then reassembling the particles cannot restore the person, because the continuity that made them who they were is gone. If a person is more than matter, then no laboratory can reach what needs to be raised. Either way, the method cannot do what the promise requires. A second objection needs no theology. Fedorov never asks whether the dead want to come back. He assumes their consent, but the project is a rescue operation mounted on behalf of people who never asked to be rescued. And a third: the common task demands the whole of humanity united in a single project, directed, in Fedorov's own writings, by the Russian Czar. A plan that requires universal agreement and a single command has a failure mode, and the twentieth century supplied it more than once.

[7:18] With a century of hindsight, the picture changes, and not only against him. Start with what landed. The teenager who studied in his reading room, Tsiolkovsky, became the theorist of rocket flight, and the Soviet space program that put Sputnik overhead in nineteen fifty-seven grew in soil Fedorov had tilled. The historian Brian Harvey has shown how deep that influence ran: for Soviet citizens, Sputnik was less a shock than the realization of an old idea. The artificial organs, the solar energy, the managed Earth: all mainstream now, all proposed by a librarian in the eighteen eighties. The stranger afterlife is in the life-extension movement. Russian transhumanists founded their movement in two thousand three in his lineage, and the country's first cryonics company, KrioRus, began freezing bodies in two thousand five on the wager that future science will do what Fedorov said it must. The physicist Frank Tipler, working independently, arrived at a cosmology ending with the resurrection of everyone who ever lived, simulated rather than reassembled. Fedorov would have recognized the ambition and quarreled with the method, which is to say he would have recognized it.

[8:33] But hindsight also sharpens the objections. The historian Michael Hagemeister has argued that Russian cosmism, the movement Fedorov supposedly founded, is partly a retrospective construction, assembled in the late Soviet period to serve a nationalist story about Russian identity. If he is right, then Fedorov's influence is real but messier than the legend: less a school than a rumor that kept finding new carriers. And the deepest problem remains the one Bulgakov named. A century of biology has not made the reassembly of persons from scattered particles more plausible. What survives, then, is not the engineering. It is the moral provocation at the center: that every theory of progress writes off the dead, and that a progress worth the name would have to answer to them.

[9:23] The only portrait of Fedorov was drawn in secret, by Leonid Pasternak, in the library, because the sitter would never have agreed to pose. A thin man in a ragged coat, surrounded by books, looking up. The scholar George Young, who spent years studying him, wrote that what we should do with our Godlike power is a question that someone is going to have to answer, and that Fedorov's answer may not be the best proposed, but it is the most thorough and the deepest attempt at one.
