[0:00] Twelve men sail abroad under false flags, bringing home stolen knowledge. At home, three dozen more meet in secret to decide which discoveries mankind may see. This is not a spy novel. It is a plan for the perfect research institution, written four hundred years ago by a disgraced Lord Chancellor.

[0:20] This is A Genealogy of Technological Optimism, episode three of one hundred. We're in Act One: Why should history go anywhere?

[0:28] His name was Francis Bacon, born in fifteen sixty-one. He rose to Lord Chancellor, the highest legal office in the land. Then, in the spring of sixteen twenty-one, the House of Commons charged him with twenty-three counts of taking gifts from litigants. He confessed, was fined and briefly jailed, and was barred from office for life. He retired to his books, and died in April of sixteen twenty-six, of a chill caught, the story goes, stuffing a chicken with snow.

[1:00] The book appeared the next year, appended to the natural history Sylva Sylvarum. His chaplain William Rawley, who saw it through the press, headed it plainly: New Atlantis. A Worke unfinished. Rawley explained that Bacon meant it as the description of a college for the interpreting of nature, called Salomon's House. The promised frame of laws for the best commonwealth he never wrote. The politics is missing, and Rawley says so on the first page.

[1:30] The story is simple. A ship bound from Peru toward China and Japan is blown north into unknown waters, and fifty-one souls come ashore on Bensalem, a Christian island unknown to Europe. Shown mercy, they are housed in the Strangers' House, where the Governor tells the island's history and Joabin, a Jewish merchant, tells of the founding of Salomon's House. The narrator is granted the Feast of the Family, given to any man who lives to see thirty descendants alive at once.

[2:03] Then the heart of the book: an audience with one of the Fathers, who states the House's end in Bacon's own words. The knowledge of causes, and secret motions of things; and the enlarging of the bounds of human empire, to the effecting of all things possible. Then the facilities, the most detailed blueprint for organized research written before any existed: deep caves, high towers, great lakes, houses of optics, of sounds, of engines, of the deceits of the senses.

[2:35] Then the staff: thirty-six fellows in nine offices, Twelve Merchants of Light, sailing under the names of other nations, for our own we conceal. Three Depredators, who collect the experiments found in all books. Three Mystery-men, who gather the practices of craftsmen. Three Pioneers, who try new experiments. Three Compilers, who draw results into tables. Three Dowry-men, who draw out of experiments things of use for human life. Three Lamps, who direct new experiments of a higher light, and three Inoculators, who carry them out. And three Interpreters of Nature, who raise discoveries into greater observations, axioms, and aphorisms.

[3:15] Two long galleries: working models of the House's inventions, and statues of history's great inventors, honored like kings. Appended to the whole, the Magnalia Naturae: thirty-three great works Bacon wishes into being. The fable caps the program he had built for twenty years, the Instauratio Magna. The Advancement of Learning mapped the sciences; the Novum Organum gave the method. Nature, to be commanded, must be obeyed.

[3:41] The reception, judged as the first readers could judge it. Within a generation the fable read as prophecy. The Royal Society was founded in sixteen sixty, and its first historian, Thomas Sprat, made Bacon the patron saint of the enterprise, with Cowley's ode and a frontispiece showing Bacon among the instruments. The Invisible College took up the call, and Hartlib's circle drafted college schemes straight out of Bacon. The common claim, the one on the series' own website: Salomon's House is the template for the Royal Society, and at some distance, for every lab since.

[4:19] But scholars urge care. Michael Hunter argues the Baconian label was largely ideological cover for a fractious new society. Guido Giglioni shows Bacon was read most seriously by the Revolution's losers: Hartlib, Dury, Comenius. A sixteen sixties plan to found a college explicitly on Salomon's House failed. And one structural difference cannot be skipped. Salomon's House is a state-funded priesthood that withholds knowledge even from the state. The Royal Society was a self-governing club of gentlemen publishing results. The template holds for the idea: organized, cooperative, experimental inquiry. It fails for the institution.

[4:56] So much for the first readers. The turn begins inside the text. The Father is candid about one practice: we have consultations, which of the inventions and experiences which we have discovered shall be published, and which not; and take all an oath of secrecy. Thirty-six men decide in secret what mankind may know, and tell the state only what they choose. Who governs the governors? Bensalem has no parliament, no press, and the frame of laws was never written. Nothing checks them. This is the charter document for every later argument that technical elites should decide what the public may know.

[5:37] A second objection needs no conspiracy. Adorno and Horkheimer made Bacon the figure where enlightened reason narrows into instrumental reason: knowledge valued only as power, nature reduced to manipulable stuff, and eventually human beings with it. C. S. Lewis put it as an epigram in The Abolition of Man: What we call Man's power over Nature turns out to be a power exercised by some men over other men with Nature as its instrument. Conquer nature, and the conquerors will turn the instruments on human nature itself.

[6:12] A third objection is about the language. Keller and Merchant argued Baconian science speaks in metaphors of a masculine mind interrogating a feminine nature. Keller quotes Bacon's chaste and lawful marriage between mind and nature, meant to bind nature and make her serve. The book supplies its own exhibit: at the Feast of the Family the mother waits behind a concealed door. Brian Vickers has answered that the indictment misquotes Bacon and rips him from context. The dispute is live. It is reported here, not settled.

[6:46] Then the working scientists' verdict. William Harvey, who found the circulation of the blood, told Aubrey he admired the wit but would not allow him to be a great philosopher. He writes philosophy, Harvey said, like a Lord Chancellor. Bacon prescribed the method brilliantly. He never did the science.

[7:06] With hindsight, the picture changes, and not only against him. What landed: the research university, the national laboratory, Los Alamos in nineteen forty-three the archetype. Permanent, state-funded institutions for the effecting of all things possible. In nineteen forty-five, Vannevar Bush's Science, The Endless Frontier built the National Science Foundation and installed the linear model as federal policy. Pure Baconianism, with a federal budget. In nineteen fifty-eight, after Sputnik, came ARPA. Salomon's House with a Pentagon budget.

[7:36] But hindsight sharpens the objections. Bacon's consultation on what shall be published is now standing practice: classified research, corporate secrecy, AI capabilities work, with no Fathers' benevolence to assume. And the linear model has its critics. Donald Stokes, in Pasteur's Quadrant, argued Bush's spectrum from basic to applied misdescribes research. The most fertile science, Pasteur's included, was use-inspired, the quadrant the linear model erases. The Dowry-men's work, inside Salomon's House all along. And the division of labor has its failure modes: the replication crisis, publish-or-perish. Compilers rewarded for output rather than truth.

[8:13] One correction, since the slogan will come up. Knowledge is power is not quite Bacon's line. What he wrote was nam et ipsa scientia potestas est: for knowledge itself is power, and of God's knowledge, not man's. The crisp scientia potentia est first appears in the Latin Leviathan of Thomas Hobbes, once Bacon's young secretary. The most famous sentence of Baconianism was compressed by an employee.

[8:40] Rawley called it A Worke unfinished, and the missing part is the tell. Bacon finished the blueprint down to the staffing table. The frame of laws, the part that would have said who the Fathers answer to, he never wrote. Four hundred years later, we live inside the half he finished, and still wait on the half he didn't.
